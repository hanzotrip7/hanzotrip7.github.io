A Quiet Network,
A Loud Lesson.

My first penetration test and everything I thought I knew about being ready.

June Private School 8 min read

It was June. The kind of June where everything slows down, especially at a school. No students. No teachers. I was at my desk, staring at a terminal, hands on keyboard, and a scope document that suddenly felt very real. Somewhere on the other side of that connection was a private school's network, quiet and waiting.

This was my first real penetration test. Not a lab. Not a CTF. Not a practice range with intentionally vulnerable machines. A real client. A real scope. A real responsibility. Horror and excitement rolled into one feeling.

The Engagement

The client was a private school and they wanted to understand their exposure. A reasonable ask. However, June meant staff and students were off for summer break, which meant reduced network activity. The assessment was fully remote: just me, my tools, and whatever I could reach across the wire.

From the start, something felt off. This was not your typical Windows-heavy environment. It was predominantly macOS.

That changed how I had to think because my experience mostly centered around Windows and Active Directory. Different controls. Different assumptions. You adapt or you miss things.

What I Didn’t Have

I had no real-world testing methodology. I was familiar with frameworks like PTES and OWASP, but there was a gap between labs, CTFs, and actually executing a real assessment under pressure, within a time box, with a real client waiting for a deliverable.

I remember being nervous. What if I don’t find anything? I didn’t have a reliable cheat sheet. I didn’t have a personal runbook. I was stitching together commands from memory and half-remembered blog posts, and that cost me time and confidence.

What I did have was access, a scope, and enough professional obligation to keep going even when my internal monologue was telling me I had no business being there.

So I started testing. Carefully. Slowly. Second-guessing everything.

The Findings, or the Lack Thereof

I came back with very little. A handful of observations. Nothing dramatic. No critical vulnerabilities that would make a good war story.

Then it hit me: I had very little to show for it. No major findings. No big wins. Nothing that felt like it justified me being there.

That was the moment the imposter syndrome kicked in. I remember thinking, maybe I’m not cut out for this.

There was a loneliness to it all. Just you, a terminal, and a blinking cursor, quietly wondering if the organization that hired you made a mistake.

I didn’t spiral. I kept working. But that internal voice was loud: You don’t know enough. You should have found more. A real pentester would’ve had this wrapped up by noon.

None of that was helpful. Most of it wasn’t even accurate. But it was there.

Here’s the part I didn’t understand at the time:

A penetration test is not measured by how many vulnerabilities you find. It is measured by how accurately you assess risk within scope, using a structured and safe approach.

A low-finding report does not mean you failed. It might mean the organization is doing something right. Your job is to look honestly, not to manufacture drama.

Every environment differs. Some organizations have limited security budgets or limited resources, and they are still trying to do the right thing. That deserves honesty.

What That Experience Gave Me

1. My First Cheat Sheet

After that assessment, I built something I wish I had from day one: a cheat sheet.

Not just commands. Not just tools. A structured way to think:

  • What do I check first?
  • What do I validate?
  • What am I likely to forget under pressure?

That document became my anchor. Over time, it grew into a living methodology—added to after every engagement, refined with each new environment, and more valuable than any single finding.

It’s never finished. That’s the point.

Because consistency is what separates guessing from testing.

2. The “Try Harder” Trap

You just need to try harder.

At first, it felt motivating. Push more. Do more. Never miss anything again. But that mindset can go sideways fast, and it did.

Good penetration testing is not about brute-force effort or flooding a network with traffic. It is about disciplined, scoped, and methodical work.

You do not win by throwing everything at a network or exhausting yourself. You win by thinking clearly and following a process.

I left that engagement with a “try harder” obsession that, in hindsight, was toxic. The drive to never feel underprepared again pushed me hard.

3. Redefining What “Winning” Means

That first test felt like a loss, but over time I learned something important.

You are not there to “break things” with RCEs and popped shells. You are there to tell the truth about risk.

Some environments are well secured. Some organizations are doing the right things. When that happens, fewer findings do not mean failure. They often reflect maturity.

I have “lost” plenty of assessments since this one. Hardened environments. Thin scopes. Engagements where the organization was genuinely doing well.

I have made peace with that. Organizations that pursue penetration testing are trying to do the right thing. You cannot win every assessment, and winning was never really the point.

Looking Back

I’m grateful for that engagement. Not in spite of feeling defeated, but because of it. That feeling was information. It told me to build better systems.

I never wanted to feel that defeated again. I won’t pretend I haven’t felt it since, because I have. Many times. But now it is different. Now I understand that every assessment is part of a larger process— for me and for clients.

Every organization that hires a pentester is extending a form of trust. They are saying: look at us honestly and tell us what you see.

That obligation does not get smaller when the findings are thin. That quiet network in June did not give me a highlight moment. It gave me something better: a foundation.

If you are early in your career and feeling the same way I did—uncertain, underprepared, questioning yourself—understand this:

You’re not behind. You’re building. And that first uncomfortable experience is where it starts.

Happy hacking!
